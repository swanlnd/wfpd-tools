Buying the Dips, Automatically

Invest the same amount every month and something clever happens by itself: your money buys more units when the price is low and fewer when it's high — so you never have to guess the bottom. This is "dollar-cost averaging."

Your average cost: — vs average price —

Because your fixed amount bought more units when the price was low, you paid less per unit than the market's average — without ever trying to time it.

Average cost is what you actually paid per unit — your break-even. If today's price is above it, you're ahead; below it, you're down on paper (and buying more is cheaper). Average price is just the market's plain average over the same months. Steady buying scoops up extra units on the dips, so your average cost lands below the average price — and that gap is the edge you got without timing anything.

The takeaway: steady monthly investing turns a bumpy market into an advantage — the dips become discounts, and you're never forced to guess the perfect moment. That's the same thing that happens when you invest into an ETF from your salary each month.

But it's not magic: dollar-cost averaging lowers your average cost and removes the guesswork — it does not guarantee a profit. If the price ends below what you paid (try "Long decline"), you can still be down. Prices here are simplified illustrations, not real market data or a forecast.
